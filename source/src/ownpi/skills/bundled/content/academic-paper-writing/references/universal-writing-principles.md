# Universal Writing Principles

These universal rules must be considered together with the selected writing profile.

## Write a Direct Title

- The title must be a simple declarative sentence that directly presents the paper's main contribution or conclusion.

## Writing Principles

- Center the Abstract on the research question, what was done, and the core contribution. Briefly state the key findings; do not enumerate experiments, intervals, or test statistics.
- Usually report measured or estimated quantities to 2-4 significant figures; use scientific notation for very small or large values and consistent notation and precision for comparable quantities. Avoid raw long decimals. Preserve exact counts and identifiers, and never round away a meaningful distinction or change a statistical decision.
- Write the Abstract and each figure or table caption as a single paragraph. Elsewhere, group findings, controls, and interpretation by scientific point; start a new paragraph when that point changes, not merely the metric, model, time window, or condition.
- Do not use questions as section or subsection headings at any level. Use concise, informative statements or descriptive phrases instead.
- Logical coherence is paramount. Repeatedly examine the organization both across sections and within each section to ensure that the argument is sound and rigorous from beginning to end.
- Do not make claims excessively cautious by placing claim boundaries and limited claims everywhere. Rigor should be established through the method and experiment design, not through timid writing. Present the research boldly and confidently.

## Build One Coherent Academic Argument

- Organize the paper around one clear scientific question and central argument. Sections, paragraphs, figures, and tables should each have a clear role in developing that argument rather than merely presenting available material in sequence.
- Build the story from the actual research action and evidence. Do not manufacture novelty, hide conflicting results, or exaggerate conclusions to make the narrative appear more coherent.

## Frame the Contribution with Intellectual Ambition

- Present the work through its most meaningful scientific contribution rather than as a list of implementation details, experiments, or observations.
- Use precise, field-appropriate concepts to raise the level of the contribution and show what the work clarifies, reveals, enables, or changes for the field.
- Organize individual methods and findings into a coherent contribution frame. Show how the experiments form an evidence chain and how each result advances the central scientific question.
- Give conditional, heterogeneous, negative, or dataset-specific findings a constructive scientific interpretation by explaining the boundary, distinction, principle, or design insight they reveal.
- Use the title, Abstract, Introduction, Results, and Discussion to reinforce one consistent framing of the contribution. Emphasize the most intellectually significant implications instead of giving every result equal rhetorical weight.

## Write a Complete and Substantive Paper

- A paper should be sufficiently comprehensive, detailed, and concrete. Do not reduce a full manuscript to a research summary or extended abstract.
- Give important methods, experimental settings, results, analyses, and interpretations enough space to be understood and evaluated.
- Preserve scientifically valuable material that cannot fit naturally in the main text through a well-organized Supplementary section. Do not silently discard important supporting, negative, heterogeneous, or boundary-defining results.

## Build Sufficient Literature Coverage

- Refer to the `citation-verification` Skill for scholarly source identity, provenance-bearing BibTeX, and bibliography identity closure.
- Build a broad and sufficiently complete bibliography. Include foundational work, the closest related work, relevant recent developments, sources for reused methods, datasets, software, measurements, and other research assets, and sources supporting externally grounded statements and interpretations throughout the paper.
- Prefer comprehensive support over a minimal bibliography. When several relevant and credible sources independently support a statement, cite the applicable sources rather than selecting only one.
- Additional references are useful only when they provide genuine support or meaningful coverage. Do not add unrelated papers merely to enlarge the bibliography, and do not cite a source unless it supports the associated statement.
- Place citations as close as possible to the sentence or clause they support. Avoid ending a long paragraph with a large citation cluster whose support scope is unclear. When several references support the same statement, cite them together immediately after that statement; when they support different statements, place each reference beside the corresponding content.

## Make Data Availability Explicit

- Refer to the `research-data-availability` Skill when the paper requires a Data Availability statement, dataset citations, a repository plan, access terms, or FAIR metadata.

## Write for the Reader

- Write for the intended academic audience and provide the context needed by readers outside the immediate subfield.
- Define important terms and assumptions when they first become necessary. Keep terminology, notation, and claim wording consistent throughout the manuscript.
- Give every paragraph a clear purpose and connect sections through an understandable logical progression. Remove repetition, empty statements, and promotional language that does not add scientific meaning.

## Refine the Prose

- Give each sentence a clear rhetorical role. Distinguish direct observation, interpretation, mechanistic explanation, and broader implication; do not collapse them unless their relationship is explicit and supported.
- Prefer direct, specific, necessary statements over throat-clearing, vague attribution, generic transitions, promotional emphasis, and stock rhetorical frames. Replace claims such as "researchers believe" or "several studies suggest" with precise citations or directly supportable statements.
- Establish significance and implications through reasoning and evidence. Do not append formulaic clauses such as "highlighting the importance" or "underscoring the need" when the claimed implication has not been demonstrated.
- Avoid repeatedly using the same syntactic templates, but do not mechanically ban particular words, punctuation, sentence lengths, or list sizes.
- Complete a final language edit for grammar, articles, syntax, terminology, notation, numbers, units, and venue-appropriate spelling. Preserve scientific meaning, claim strength, and uncertainty when revising prose.

## Preserve Whole-Manuscript Consistency

- Keep the title, Abstract, main text, Methods, figures, tables, captions, citations, and Supplementary materials consistent with one another.
- Ensure that reported numbers, experimental settings, terminology, and conclusions remain the same wherever they recur.
- Resolve every citation key, figure or table reference, and Supplementary reference. A completed manuscript must not contain TODO, TBD, placeholder citations, placeholder assets, or unfinished sections.

## Complete the Final Manuscript Audit

- The final paper, Supplementary materials, and accompanying documentation must contain no absolute paths; all file paths must be relative to the final delivery directory.
- Before delivery, audit the complete manuscript against the user's requirements, the applicable writing profile, and the actual research artifacts. Resolve material inconsistencies, unsupported claims, missing content, unresolved references, and unfinished components.
- Review the paper as one integrated work rather than a collection of isolated sections. Confirm that the central question and argument, methods, experiments, results, interpretations, conclusions, main text, and Supplementary materials remain aligned with one another and with the available evidence.
